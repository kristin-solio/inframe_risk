Your lender will not release money until Builder’s Risk is in place.

And not just any policy. The loan documents describe a specific one, down to the limit basis, the capacity the lender is named in, and whether soft costs are covered. Projects rarely stall because nobody bought Builder’s Risk. They stall because what was bought does not match the paper it has to satisfy.

The policy does not match the loan documents

A limit written to hard costs when the loan is underwritten to completed value. The lender added as an additional insured when the documents call for mortgagee and loss payee. No soft costs, when interest carry and lost rent are the exposure the lender is actually protecting. Good insurance. Not the policy the documents describe.

The contract and the policy disagree

Your construction contract already says who buys Builder’s Risk, who is named on it, who absorbs the deductible after a loss, and whether subrogation is waived against the subs. Coverage bought without reading that clause is coverage two parties argue over later, while they are both still on the job.

A certificate holds up a draw

Evidence issued to the wrong borrowing entity. An endorsement the lender asked for that has not come back from the carrier. A draw package sitting while somebody chases a document. We handle certificates and changes as close to real time as possible, so the schedule is not waiting on us.

Builder’s Risk questions

Who buys Builder’s Risk on a project, the owner or the contractor?

Either, and the construction contract is what settles it. On many commercial and multifamily projects the owner or developer procures Builder’s Risk, often because the construction loan requires the borrowing entity to carry it, and the general contractor is named on the policy. On others the contract puts the obligation on the general contractor. The practical risk is both parties assuming the other bought it, or both buying it. We read the contract and the loan documents first, so the project has one clear answer.

What do lenders usually require?

Requirements vary by lender and by loan, so the loan documents are the authority. In practice lenders commonly look for coverage in place before funds are advanced, a limit that reflects the full completed value of the work, the lender named in the right capacity, and the borrowing entity and contractor named as the documents describe. Many construction loans also call for soft costs and delay in completion coverage, because a loss that pushes the schedule costs the lender interest and lost rent, not just repairs. We check your policy line by line against what your documents actually say.

When should Builder’s Risk be in place?

Before work starts and before materials arrive on site, and on a financed project usually earlier than that, because the lender wants evidence of coverage at or before closing and will often not release the first draw without it. Builder’s Risk is frequently the gating item on a construction loan, so it is worth starting while the loan is still being documented rather than the week before the first mobilization.

We are renovating an existing building. What covers the building itself?

Usually not the Builder’s Risk policy. Builder’s Risk is generally written to cover the work being performed and the materials going into it, not the structure that was already standing. The existing building normally stays on the owner’s property policy, and on a financed renovation a lender will often want evidence of both, plus a clear answer on which policy responds when a single loss damages the new work and the existing structure together. It is one of the most common gaps on renovation deals, and it is a far better conversation before the first draw than after a loss.

What happens if the project runs past the policy term?

Builder’s Risk is written for a fixed term tied to the schedule, not until the job is finished. If the schedule slips, the policy has to be extended, and an extension is not automatic: the carrier has to agree to it, it is usually priced, and it is a much harder conversation after the term has lapsed than before. Tell your broker as soon as the schedule moves. A project sitting uninsured between expiry and extension is a problem for you and an immediate one for your lender.

Can we start leasing or occupying before the project is finished?

Not without telling the insurer first. Most Builder’s Risk forms restrict or end coverage once a building is occupied, which is a live issue on multifamily, where lease-up often starts while the last units and the site work are still in progress. Partial occupancy generally needs an endorsement agreed in advance. Moving tenants in without one can leave the project uninsured at the point the most value is standing on it.

What is the difference between an installation floater and Builder’s Risk?

Builder’s Risk covers the project under construction, typically the structure and the materials that will become part of it, and it is usually bought by the owner or the general contractor for the whole job. An installation floater covers one trade contractor’s own interest in the materials and equipment they are installing, while those are in transit, in temporary storage and on site, up to the point the work is installed and accepted. Trade contractors often need a floater even on a project where someone else carries Builder’s Risk. Which one you need, and what each actually covers, comes down to the policy form and your contract.

What project types does InFrame place Builder’s Risk for?

Ground-up commercial, multifamily, and commercial renovation and retrofit work, across all 50 states and DC. Do you cover a renovation on my own home?

No, and we would rather say so here than take up your time in a form. InFrame places insurance for commercial and multifamily construction. For a renovation or a new build on a home you own and live in, the right first call is your own homeowners insurer or a personal lines agent, who can look at whether your existing policy covers the work or whether you need a separate course of construction policy.
